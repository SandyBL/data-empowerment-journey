If one of them looks unfinished, that's the point: the finishing is the work.

Take them, rename them, put your logo on them and never mention where they came from. That's what they're for.

## How to use them without building a museum

The failure mode with templates isn't abandonment. It's completion. A team fills in every cell, circulates the file, receives no comments, and files it — and the exercise has produced a document instead of a decision. I've watched that happen with my own templates, which is why this section exists.

Three habits stop it.

**Fill them in a room, not at a desk.** Almost all of these are conversation scaffolds. The pain-point sheet is worth ten times more filled in badly by six people from four departments than filled in perfectly by you, because the argument about which row belongs where *is* the finding. Your tidy version is a guess with good formatting.

**Leave the disagreements visible.** If two functions define "active customer" differently, don't quietly reconcile it to keep the document clean. Write both definitions in the cell. That contradiction is the strongest evidence you will ever have that a [business glossary](/en/glossary/business-glossary/) is needed, and it evaporates the moment somebody smooths it over to look competent.

**Cut the columns you won't maintain.** A governance scorecard with nine metrics nobody updates is worse than one with three that get reviewed every month, because the abandoned version teaches the organization that governance artifacts go stale. Delete aggressively before you circulate, not apologetically six months later.

## What each one is for

The **pain-point identification** sheet is where most of my projects start: it collects specific failures — a report nobody trusts, a reconciliation that eats two days a month — and traces each one back to the process that creates it. Pair it with the [cost calculator](/en/calculator/) and price the top three.

The **5W2H framing** document is for the moment somebody senior asks "so what is this program, exactly?" and you need one page rather than a deck. It's the most-used file here, which tells you how often that moment arrives without warning.

**Governance evolution stages** describes what each maturity level looks like in behavior rather than in adjectives, which is what makes a [maturity assessment](/en/maturity-assessment/) result mean something to a sponsor who has never heard of DAMA.

**Catalog tool use cases** exists to stop you buying a catalog before you know what question it's meant to answer. Fill it in before the demos, not after. Afterward it becomes a justification exercise, and you'll notice yourself being generous.

**Principles and policies** gives you the shape of a short [data policy](/en/glossary/data-policy/) set — the kind people can actually recall — instead of a forty-page document that gets approved and never read.

The **governance scorecard** is the standing report: a small set of measures that tells a council whether the operating model is working, or whether it's just meeting.
