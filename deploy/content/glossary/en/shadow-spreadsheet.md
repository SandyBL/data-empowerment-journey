Every organization has them: the export that finance re-keys every month, the regional workbook with its own definition of "active customer", the file the analyst trusts more than the dashboard. Each one started as a reasonable workaround for a real problem — slow access, a wrong number, a report that did not answer the question. Together they are where the organization's actual numbers live, outside any control, lineage or audit.

**In practice.** Treat shadow spreadsheets as evidence, not misconduct. Ask what each one fixes, and you have a ranked list of data pain points: missing definitions, access that takes three weeks, quality nobody escalates. Counting them is a reasonable proxy for the trust gap. Retire them by fixing the reason they exist and giving consumers a route to report discrepancies to a steward, then measure how many go away.

**Where it goes wrong.** Governance bans spreadsheets or locks down exports without fixing what drove people to them. The work moves somewhere less visible, the risk is relocated rather than reduced, and the next audit finds the same numbers in a personal drive. A spreadsheet nobody needs anymore disappears on its own.
