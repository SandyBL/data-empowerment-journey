DCAM is published by the EDM Council (Enterprise Data Management Council), an industry association with deep roots in financial services, and it shows: it is built for organizations that will one day have to show an auditor or a regulator how their data is managed. It groups data management into eight capability areas — strategy and business case, program and funding, business and technical architecture, data quality, governance, the control environment and analytics — and scores each against six realization levels, from not initiated to enhanced. Where DAMA-DMBOK describes what data management covers, DCAM is designed to measure how far along you are.

**In practice.** Its strength is evidence. A DCAM score is meant to rest on artefacts — the approved policy, the funded role, the control that runs — which suits it to results that must survive an audit or be compared across business units. It is also detailed, and full use generally runs through the Council's membership and training. Most smaller programs borrow its structure rather than running it end to end.

**Where it goes wrong.** DCAM is chosen because it sounds rigorous, then run as a self-scored spreadsheet by the team being assessed. The detail that makes it credible becomes the reason it never finishes. If nobody outside the data office will look at the scores, a lighter model and three named actions will do more.
