Certification is a promise attached to a specific asset. Somebody accountable has checked the definition, the source and the quality, and puts their name to it. The badge in the catalog or the BI tool is what makes that promise usable. It lets a reader make a trust decision in a second instead of an hour, and it is what separates the governed margin figure from the four ad-hoc versions next to it. Some organizations use tiers, such as gold for decision-grade data, rather than a single yes or no.

**In practice.** Certify a few things that matter and make the criteria public: owner, approved definition, quality inside tolerance, known consumers. Treat retirement as a decision too, made by the metric owner with the listed consumers consulted and recent usage as evidence. Measure whether certified assets are reused rather than rebuilt, and whether people are still using them after the first 90 days.

**Where it goes wrong.** Certification is granted once and never reviewed, so the badge outlives the owner, the definition and the quality checks behind it. The opposite failure is campaigning for people to prefer certified data before any exists. Either way the badge stops meaning anything, and people go back to their own spreadsheets.
