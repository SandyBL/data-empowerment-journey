Governance workshops your team plays, not sits through

I run your workshop on the same scenario simulators this site publishes — rebranded, rewritten around your systems and your teams, on a private leaderboard — and you keep the facilitator report.

Last updated September 5, 2026

Everyone has sat through the governance training. Someone presents the DMBOK wheel, defines a data steward, shows a maturity ladder, and asks for questions. Nobody has any, because nothing was at stake. Two weeks later the only measurable outcome is the attendance list.

The problem is not the content. It is that governance is a discipline of trade-offs, and you cannot teach a trade-off by describing it. You have to make someone choose, show them what their choice cost, and then have the argument.

That is what this format does.

The format

A session runs two to three hours for a group of eight to forty. It alternates between short framing — twenty minutes on decision rights, on quality dimensions, on what an owner actually owns — and rounds on a scenario simulator that the whole room plays on their phones.

Each round puts the group inside a situation with no clean answer. Two departments both claim ownership of the customer record. A regulator asks where a number came from and the lineage stops at a spreadsheet. A quality rule is failing 14% of the time and fixing the source means asking another director to change a process they are measured on. Everyone chooses, the choices are scored against governance consequence rather than correctness, and the leaderboard goes up on the screen.

Then we debrief the disagreements, which is where the real session happens. People argue about their own organisation, using their own examples, having just discovered that their colleagues would have decided differently.

Your private, branded space

This is the part that does not exist anywhere else. The three simulators on this site are public — one worldwide leaderboard, generic scenarios, and nothing to take away afterwards; what those public boards show is a summary of everyone's runs and a set of general lessons, not a reading of any one organisation. For a workshop I open a private space for your organisation instead.

What you keep

The facilitator report is the artefact that makes this more than a good afternoon. It ranks the dimensions your group was weakest on, first and most prominently, with maturity bands and per-question timings. That is a diagnosis of your organisation's governance instincts, produced by forty people making choices under mild time pressure rather than by a survey they had time to game.

In practice it does two things. It tells you where to spend the next quarter's effort, and it gives you something to show a sponsor: not "the team enjoyed the workshop", but "here are the three areas where our own people did not know who decides, ranked".

You also keep the CSV export, and the space stays open for the length of the licence — so a second cohort, a follow-up session, or the same team retaking it after six months all land on the same leaderboard and become a comparison.

Which simulator fits

There are three scenarios, and they suit different rooms.

  • Data Governance Day-to-Day — the week of a governance lead: competing requests, a failing rule, an escalation, a definition nobody agrees on. Best for a governance team, a steward network, or a leadership group that needs to feel what the role involves.
  • Who Owns This? — a disputed ownership claim between two departments, played out to the end. Best when ownership is the actual blocker, and especially good with the two departments in the room together.
  • Data Literacy — raising literacy with no training budget. Best for a broad business audience rather than a data team.

A three-hour session usually uses one scenario in depth; a full-day programme uses two.

Practical details

Sessions run remotely or on site, in English, Spanish or Portuguese — and the same session can mix all three, because each participant picks their own language and they all share one leaderboard. Participants need a phone or laptop and a browser. There are no accounts, no installs and no app: one access code for the whole room.
