How to use them without making a museum

The failure mode with templates is completion. A team fills every cell, circulates the file, receives no comments, and files it — and the exercise has produced a document instead of a decision.

Three habits stop that.

Fill them in a room, not at a desk. Almost all of these are conversation scaffolds. The pain-point sheet is worth ten times more filled in badly by six people from four departments than filled in perfectly by you, because the argument about which row belongs where is the finding.

Leave the disagreements visible. If two functions define "active customer" differently, do not reconcile it silently to keep the document tidy. Write both definitions in the cell. That contradiction is the strongest evidence you will ever have that a business glossary is needed, and it evaporates the moment somebody smooths it over.

Cut the columns you will not maintain. A governance scorecard with nine metrics that nobody updates is worse than one with three that get reviewed monthly, because the abandoned version teaches the organisation that governance artefacts go stale. Delete aggressively before you circulate.

What each one is for

The pain-point identification sheet is where most engagements start: it collects specific failures — a report nobody trusts, a reconciliation that eats two days a month — and traces each to the process that creates it. Pair it with the cost calculator to price the top three.

The 5W2H framing document is for the moment somebody asks "so what is this programme, exactly?" and you need one page rather than a deck.

Governance evolution stages describes what each maturity level actually looks like in behaviour, which is what makes a maturity assessment result mean something to a sponsor.

Catalogue tool use cases exists to stop you buying a catalogue before you know what question it is meant to answer. Fill it in before the demos, not after.

Principles and policies gives you the shape of a short data policy set — the kind people can actually recall — rather than a forty-page document that gets approved and never read.

The governance scorecard is the standing report: a small set of measures that tells a council whether the operating model is working.
