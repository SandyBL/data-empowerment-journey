"Our data quality is poor" has never funded anything. It is a complaint, and every executive has heard a version of it from every function. What funds work is a number with a method behind it.

That is what this does. Put in what you know — how many people, how much time, how often — and it returns an annual figure you can defend line by line.

How to use the number

The output is an estimate, and its value is not precision. Its value is that it is yours, derived from processes your colleagues recognise, which means it survives the only question that matters: where did that come from.

That is why it beats the benchmark. Every vendor deck quotes some version of "bad data costs companies 15–25% of revenue". A CFO discounts that figure in one sentence, correctly, because it describes an average of organisations that are nothing like yours. A modest number derived from your own reconciliation hours does not have that weakness.

Present it as a range, not a point. Give the low and high end and name the assumption that moves it. Nobody trusts a data quality cost quoted to the nearest euro, and offering the range yourself pre-empts the objection.

Attach it to a specific fix. The figure on its own invites scepticism about the method. The figure next to "and here is the one control that would remove most of it, which costs this much" invites a decision. Governance gets funded as the cheaper of two numbers, never as a good idea.

Recalculate after the fix. The second most persuasive thing you can produce is the same calculation six months later with a lower answer. That is what turns a one-off business case into a standing budget line.

What it deliberately leaves out

There is no attempt here to price reputational damage, opportunity cost or the decisions your organisation did not make because it did not trust the report. Those are real and often larger than the operational cost, but they cannot be estimated defensibly, and including a soft number next to a hard one contaminates both.

Similarly, this measures the cost of the current state, not the return on a governance programme. Those are different claims and conflating them is how business cases fall apart under scrutiny. Cost of the problem: this calculator. Cost of the fix: a scope and a quote.
